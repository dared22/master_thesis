\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

Financial time series are commonly modelled as realizations of stochastic
processes because their future evolution is uncertain \parencite{PishroNik2014}.
This section first introduces ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to describe
continuous-time dynamics and stochastic processes to describe randomness. It
then brings these ideas together in stochastic differential equations (SDEs),
which describe how a system evolves under both systematic and random
influences. For the financial data considered here, the aim is to learn these
dynamics from observed time series. SDEs can be fitted to discrete observations,
including sparsely sampled data, although sparse observations can make the
underlying dynamics difficult to identify \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.
Neural SDEs use neural networks to learn unknown dynamics from data
\parencite{Kidger2022}.
\bgroup\markoverwith{\textcolor{yellow}{\rule[-0.5ex]{2pt}{2.5ex}}}\ULon{The
section develops these concepts toward the thesis's aim of generating
equity-return paths and assessing their dependence and risk characteristics.}

\subsection{Ordinary differential equations}
\label{sec:ode-theory}

An ordinary differential equation (ODE) specifies how a state
$x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ changes over time. Together with a starting value,
it forms an initial-value problem \parencite{kohler2014elementary}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dx(t)}{dt}=f(x(t),u(t),t),\qquad x(t_0)=x_0.
    \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
The state contains the $d$ quantities describing the system, $u(t)$ is a known
input with no randomness, and $f$ gives the rate of change. We later refer to
$f$ as the \emph{vector field}: it gives the direction and rate of change at
each state for a given input and time. Integrating this rate gives the
starting state plus the accumulated change
\parencite{thomas2003thomas}:
\begin{equation}
    x(t)=x_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}f(x(s),u(s),s)\,ds.
    \label{eq:ode-integral}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Numerical approximation}

Euler's method approximates the solution by taking steps using the rate of
change at the start of each step \parencite{kohler2014elementary}:
\begin{equation}
    x_{i+1}=x_i+h_i f(x_i,u(t_i),t_i),\qquad h_i=t_{i+1}-t_i,
    \label{eq:euler-ode}
\end{equation}
where $x_i$ approximates $x(t_i)$ and $h_i$ is the step size. For example,
$dx/dt=2x$ with $x(0)=1$ has the exact solution $x(t)=e^{2t}$, while Euler
uses $x_{i+1}=(1+2h)x_i$ for a constant step size $h$.
Figure~\ref{fig:ode-euler} shows how smaller steps improve this approximation.
For sufficiently smooth solutions and small steps, halving $h$ roughly halves
the error over a fixed time interval \parencite{kohler2014elementary}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/theory/ode-euler.pdf}
    \caption{Exact solution of $dx/dt=2x$, $x(0)=1$, and Euler approximations
    with three step sizes $h$. Markers show computed values; straight lines
    connect them. The vertical line marks the error $\epsilon$ between the

    \label{fig:ode-euler}
\end{figure}

The solver may take several steps between observations. Smaller steps can
improve numerical accuracy, but do not add data for learning the model.

\subsection{Stochastic processes}
\label{sec:stochastic-processes}

A stochastic process is a family of random variables indexed by time, written
$\{X_t:t\in\mathcal{T}\}$. At a fixed time $t$, $X_t$ is a random variable
describing the possible states at that time. One outcome of the process across
time is called a realization or sample path \parencite{PishroNik2014}.
The model therefore describes a probability distribution over possible paths,
including the distribution of values at each time and their dependence across
time \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}. An observed time series records values from
just one realization.

For a continuous-time process, the time index ranges over an interval, such as
$\mathcal{T}=[0,T]$; for a discrete-time process, it ranges over a countable set.
Continuous time alone does not require continuous sample paths
\parencite{PishroNik2014}.
If the state is observed without measurement error, the data are
\begin{equation}
    y_i=X_{t_i},\qquad i=0,\ldots,n.
    \label{eq:process-observations}
\end{equation}
The observation times may be regularly or irregularly spaced. Daily values
sample a path without revealing its within-day evolution. Generating paths
therefore requires a model for how the state evolves between observations.

\subsubsection{Brownian motion}
\label{sec:brownian-motion}

Brownian motion, also called a Wiener process, models accumulated random
changes. Standard Brownian motion $W_t$ starts at $W_0=0$ and has continuous
paths. An increment $W_{t+h}-W_t$ is the change over a time interval of length
$h$. Increments over non-overlapping intervals are independent and satisfy
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}:
\begin{equation}
    W_{t+h}-W_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,h),\qquad h>0.
    \label{eq:brownian-increments}
\end{equation}
Throughout this thesis, $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$ denotes a normal
distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Thus the increment has
mean zero, variance $h$, and standard deviation $\sqrt{h}$. Its size is
therefore scaled by $\sqrt{h}$, rather than $h$ \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.

Values of a Brownian path at chosen times can be generated by adding
independent normal increments:
\begin{equation}
    W_{t_{i+1}}=W_{t_i}+\sqrt{h_i}\,Z_i,\qquad
    h_i=t_{i+1}-t_i,\qquad Z_i\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).
    \label{eq:brownian-grid}
\end{equation}
Here the $Z_i$ are independent standard normal draws. The scaled increments
have variances $h_i$, which add to $t$ when starting at time zero. Their sum
is also normal, giving $W_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,t)$. Different draws produce
different paths of the same process \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.

Although the paths are continuous, they have no ordinary time derivative
anywhere, with probability one \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}. The SDEs below
therefore use Brownian increments to represent random changes.

\subsection{Stochastic differential equations}
\label{sec:sde-theory}

An SDE combines the rate-of-change description of an ODE with a stochastic
driving process. In the It\^o convention, a scalar diffusion driven by Brownian
motion satisfies
\begin{equation}
    dX_t=b(t,X_t)\,dt+s(t,X_t)\,dW_t,
    \label{eq:sde}
\end{equation}
where $b$ is the drift, $s$ is the diffusion amplitude, and $W_t$ is the
Brownian motion introduced in Section~\ref{sec:brownian-motion}.
The drift describes the systematic contribution to the evolution, while the
diffusion amplitude controls the strength of the random forcing
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}. The solution $X_t$ is itself a
stochastic process: each realization of the driving Brownian motion generates
a state trajectory for a given initial condition when a unique solution exists.

Since Brownian paths are not differentiable, we use the integral form
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019},
\begin{equation}
    X_t=X_0+\int_0^t b(u,X_u)\,du
        +\int_0^t s(u,X_u)\,dW_u.
    \label{eq:sde-integral}
\end{equation}

Measurements may also contain noise. A simple observation model is
\begin{equation}
    x_{\mathrm{obs}}(t_i)=X_{t_i}+\epsilon_i,
    \label{eq:noisy-observations}
\end{equation}
where $X_{t_i}$ is the underlying state and $\epsilon_i$ is measurement noise.
This noise affects the recorded value, while the Brownian term in an SDE
affects how the state evolves. An ODE can therefore have noisy observations
without its dynamics being stochastic. A model can also include both
stochastic dynamics and measurement noise \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.

\subsubsection{Euler--Maruyama and local increments}

Euler--Maruyama adds a random change to each Euler step
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}:
\begin{equation}
    X_{i+1}=X_i+b(t_i,X_i)h_i
       +s(t_i,X_i)\sqrt{h_i}\,Z_i,
    \qquad Z_i\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).
    \label{eq:euler-maruyama}
\end{equation}
Here $X_i$ approximates $X_{t_i}$, $h_i=t_{i+1}-t_i$ is the step size,
and the $Z_i$ are independent standard normal draws.

Let $\Delta X_i=X_{t_{i+1}}-X_{t_i}$ denote the state change over an interval.
The notation $\Delta X_i\mid X_{t_i}=x$ means this change \emph{given that}
the state at the start is $x$. Treating $b$ and $s$ as constant over a short
interval gives \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}
\begin{equation}
    \Delta X_i\mid X_{t_i}=x
       \approx\mathcal{N}\!\left(b(t_i,x)h_i,s(t_i,x)^2h_i\right).
    \label{eq:local-gaussian}
\end{equation}
The mean change is $b(t_i,x)h_i$ and its variance is $s(t_i,x)^2h_i$.
This distribution is exact for one Euler--Maruyama step, but generally
approximate for the SDE itself. Over a long observation interval, $b$ and $s$
may change substantially, making this approximation less reliable.

\subsubsection{Example: a randomly forced oscillator}
\label{sec:oscillator-theory}

A simple SDE example is an oscillator whose velocity receives random changes
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}:
\begin{equation}
    dX_t=V_t\,dt,\qquad
    dV_t=-\omega_0^2X_t\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t.
    \label{eq:stochastic-oscillator}
\end{equation}
Here $X_t$ is position and $V_t$ is velocity. The term $-\omega_0^2X_t$
pulls the system back towards zero, producing oscillations. The parameter
$\omega_0$ controls their speed, while $\sigma$ controls the strength of the
random changes in velocity.

Euler--Maruyama approximates both quantities using their current values
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        X_{i+1}&=X_i+hV_i,\\
        V_{i+1}&=V_i-\omega_0^2hX_i+\sigma\sqrt{h}\,Z_i,
        \qquad Z_i\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:oscillator-euler}
\end{equation}
Each step advances the position and adds a new random change to the velocity.
Figure~\ref{fig:oscillator-paths} shows ten paths from the same starting state.
Their differences arise from the stochastic dynamics; no measurement noise
is added.

\subsection{Neural stochastic differential equations}
\label{sec:neural-sde-theory}

Neural differential equations use neural networks to represent unknown
coefficient functions. The deterministic neural ODE provides a starting point
for introducing neural drift and diffusion functions in an SDE
\parencite{Kidger2022}.

\subsubsection{Neural networks}
\label{sec:neural-network-background}

A neural network is a parameterized function, written $f_\theta$, where
$\theta$ collects its trainable weights and biases. Training learns these
parameters from data by minimizing a loss, which measures how well the model
fits the data \parencite{Goodfellow2016}. In a neural SDE, networks can
represent the unknown drift
$b_\theta$ and, if needed, the diffusion $s_\theta$, allowing these functions
to be learned from observations \parencite{Kidger2022}.

This thesis assumes some familiarity with neural networks and deep learning,
including layers, activation functions, and gradient-based training. The focus
here is on using networks within differential equations.

\subsubsection{Neural ordinary differential equations}
\label{sec:neural-ode-theory}

A neural ODE replaces a prescribed vector field by a parameterized neural
network \parencite{Chen2018}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dz(t)}{dt}=f_\theta(t,z(t)),\qquad z(t_0)=z_0.
    \label{eq:neural-ode}
\end{equation}
The parameters $\theta$ determine the dynamics, while a numerical solver
evaluates the trajectory. Euler discretization gives
$z_{i+1}=z_i+h_i f_\theta(t_i,z_i)$, connecting the model to residual updates.
The continuous model separates the vector field from the solver's time grid
\parencite{Chen2018}.

For fully observed deterministic trajectories $y_j$ at times $t_j$, one
possible objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{J}(\theta)
       =\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
          \|z_\theta(t_j)-y_j\|^2.
    \label{eq:neural-ode-objective}
\end{equation}
Other measures of fit can replace squared error, and penalties can be added
to discourage overly complex models. Training adjusts the network's parameters
according to how they affect the predicted paths and the resulting loss.

With fixed parameters and initial state, a neural ODE remains deterministic.
Random initial conditions or a separate observation model can introduce
uncertainty, but the vector field alone does not add random forcing during
evolution.

\subsubsection{From neural ODEs to neural SDEs}

A neural SDE parameterizes the drift and, where required, the diffusion:
\begin{equation}
    dX_t=b_\theta(t,X_t)\,dt+s_\theta(t,X_t)\,dW_t.
    \label{eq:neural-sde}
\end{equation}
Each independent realization has its own Brownian forcing. Neural networks
provide flexible coefficient functions, but their parameterization must still
respect the conditions needed for a well-defined SDE
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}. Neural differential
equations and their stochastic extensions are developed in
\textcite{Kidger2022}.

\subsubsection{Structure used in the toy example}

For the periodic-potential experiment, the drift network receives
$(\sin x,\cos x)$ as inputs. This enforces the known period $2\pi$, appropriate
to the generating drift $b(x)=-\sin x$. Its four hidden layers each contain 64 units
with hyperbolic-tangent activation. The architecture learns the shape of the
periodic drift while incorporating prior knowledge of its period.

The active diffusion model is a single learned positive scalar, using the
softplus function \parencite{Goodfellow2016}:
\begin{equation}
    s_\theta(x)=\operatorname{softplus}(\rho)+\varepsilon,
    \qquad \operatorname{softplus}(\rho)=\log(1+e^\rho),
    \qquad \varepsilon=10^{-3}.
    \label{eq:positive-diffusion}
\end{equation}
Thus the experiments learn a neural drift and constant diffusion. Positivity
avoids the sign ambiguity of a scalar diffusion amplitude when identifying its
law through $s_\theta^2$.

\subsubsection{Likelihood training from observed increments}

For each observed increment $\Delta X_i$, the model predicts a normal
distribution with mean $b_\theta(X_i)h_i$ and variance $s_\theta(X_i)^2h_i$,
as in Equation~\eqref{eq:local-gaussian}. Evaluating its probability density
at the observed increment gives the likelihood of that increment given its
starting state. Training adjusts the parameters to increase this likelihood.
Equivalently, we minimize its negative logarithm. This is the
approximated-likelihood approach based on Euler transition densities
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.

To derive the loss, write the predicted mean as $\mu_i=b_\theta(X_i)h_i$
and the variance as $v_i=s_\theta(X_i)^2h_i$. The normal probability density
\parencite{NISTHandbook} evaluated at the observed increment is
\begin{equation*}
    p_\theta(\Delta X_i\mid X_i)
      =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v_i}}
       \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\Delta X_i-\mu_i)^2}{2v_i}\right).
\end{equation*}
Taking the negative logarithm turns the product into a sum and removes the
exponential:
\begin{equation*}
    -\log p_\theta(\Delta X_i\mid X_i)
      =\frac{(\Delta X_i-\mu_i)^2}{2v_i}
       +\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi v_i).
\end{equation*}
Substituting the mean and variance, and writing the residual as
$r_i=\Delta X_i-b_\theta(X_i)h_i$, gives the loss below. The logarithmic
term splits into two terms because $s_\theta(X_i)>0$:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac12\log(2\pi s_\theta(X_i)^2h_i)
      =\log s_\theta(X_i)+\frac12\log(2\pi h_i).
\end{equation*}
The negative log-likelihood is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_i(\theta)
       =\frac{r_i^2}{2s_\theta(X_i)^2h_i}
        +\log s_\theta(X_i)
        +\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi h_i).
    \label{eq:neural-sde-nll}
\end{equation}
The implementation averages
the first two terms in Equation~\eqref{eq:neural-sde-nll}, omitting the final
term because it does not depend on the learned parameters and therefore
does not affect training. Actual elapsed times $h_i$ enter the loss,
including when selecting a subset of saved observations. Consecutive
increments form conditional transition factors through the Markov property;
their starting states are not assumed to be independent
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.

The residual term measures agreement with the local drift and noise scale.
The $\log s_\theta(X_i)$ term prevents arbitrarily increasing diffusion to reduce the
standardized residual. Drift and diffusion are therefore coupled in training.
Adam \parencite{KingmaBa2015} performs parameter updates using randomly sampled
minibatches.
The data-size experiments select a checkpoint using a fixed sample of
held-out increments. This fitting strategy assumes direct state observations
without measurement noise; noisy or partially observed states require a
different observation model or inference procedure
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.

\subsubsection{Checking the learned model}

Because the data are simulated, we know the true drift and diffusion. We can
therefore compare them directly with the learned values. To check the drift,
we evaluate both functions at the same $Q$ evenly spaced positions $x_q$
covering one period. The percentage errors are
\begin{equation}
    E_b=100\sqrt{
       \frac{\sum_{q=1}^{Q}[b_\theta(x_q)-b(x_q)]^2}
            {\sum_{q=1}^{Q}b(x_q)^2}},\qquad
    E_\sigma=100\frac{|\widehat{\sigma}-\sigma|}{\sigma}.
    \label{eq:coefficient-errors}
\end{equation}
Here $b$ is the true drift, $b_\theta$ is the learned drift, $\sigma$ is the
true diffusion amplitude, and $\widehat{\sigma}$ is its learned value.
The drift error $E_b$ combines the differences across the sampled positions
and expresses their size relative to the true drift. The diffusion error
$E_\sigma$ is the difference between the learned and true amplitudes,
relative to the true amplitude. Zero means an exact match at the evaluated
positions for drift, or an exact match of the amplitude for diffusion.
These relative errors require at least one nonzero true drift value on the
evaluation grid and $\sigma>0$.

We also check the loss on observations not used for training and compare
the final positions of simulated trajectories. These checks answer different
questions: the loss measures how well the model explains observed increments,
while the final positions show its behavior over a longer time. A low
validation loss or similar final-position distributions do not by themselves
guarantee that the learned drift and diffusion are correct. In particular,
at short observation intervals, drift errors can have little effect on the
loss because their contribution is small relative to the noise.

The amount of data also needs to be described carefully. With $M$ trajectories
and $K$ observations per trajectory, there are $M(K-1)$ observed increments.
However, the same increment count can come from densely sampled short paths
or sparsely sampled long paths. These need not be equally useful for learning
the coefficients, so the experiments distinguish observation spacing,
trajectory duration, and the number of independent trajectories.

\subsubsection{Illustrative neural SDE example}
\label{sec:neural-example}
The following toy example illustrates likelihood training and the role of
observation spacing, trajectory duration, and training choices. It uses the
periodic-potential SDE
\begin{equation}
    dX_t=-\sin(X_t)\,dt+1.1\,dW_t,\qquad X_0=0.
\end{equation}
The model learns a periodic drift function and a positive constant diffusion
using the local Gaussian loss in Equation~\eqref{eq:neural-sde-nll}.
The comparisons below use 50 training trajectories, simulated over $T=500$
with 15,000 saved observations per path. The saved spacing is approximately
$0.03334$, with four Euler integration steps per observation interval.
Subsampling or truncating these paths changes the observation spacing or
duration while preserving the underlying numerical simulation.

Each configuration is fitted five times with new training data and training
seeds. Configurations within a repeat share the training pool and seed.
Training uses Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$, minibatches of 2,048
increments, and 1,500 updates. Checkpoints are selected using a fixed sample
of 4,096 held-out increments at the original fine observation spacing,
including the untrained model as a candidate. Coefficient errors follow
Equation~\eqref{eq:coefficient-errors}, with drift evaluated on 512 uniformly
spaced points over one period. The figures report medians and interquartile
ranges across five fits; the bands describe variation between runs, rather
than confidence intervals.

\paragraph{Observation spacing and diffusion recovery.}
\label{sec:observation-spacing-example}

At fixed duration $T=500$, increasing the number of observations per path
reduces the distance in time between observations.
Figure~\ref{fig:repeated-density} shows that median diffusion error decreases
from $18.18\%$ at spacing $\Delta t\approx1.00$ to $0.86\%$ at
$\Delta t\approx0.03334$. Drift error initially improves as the coarsest
sampling is refined, but does not continue to decrease with denser sampling:
its median is $8.45\%$ at 3,000 observations and $10.72\%$ at 15,000.
Thus, once the coarsest intervals are excluded, finer sampling benefits
diffusion recovery more consistently than drift recovery in this example.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/results/repeated-observation-density.pdf}
    \caption{Exploratory observation-density sweep at fixed duration $T=500$
    with 50 training trajectories. Lines show median coefficient errors over
    five fits; bands show the 25th--75th percentiles. Validation uses the
    original fine observation spacing for every configuration.}
    \label{fig:repeated-density}
\end{figure}

This distinction is consistent with the local increment model in
Equation~\eqref{eq:local-gaussian}: the drift contribution scales with
$\Delta t$, while the random contribution scales with $\sqrt{\Delta t}$.
Reducing the interval therefore makes the drift contribution smaller relative
to the noise in each increment. More frequent observations over the same time
span need not improve drift recovery in the same way as diffusion recovery.

\paragraph{Trajectory duration and drift recovery.}

The duration comparison keeps observation spacing approximately $0.03334$
and uses progressively longer prefixes of the same paths.
In Figure~\ref{fig:repeated-duration}, median drift error decreases overall
from $17.08\%$ at $T\approx33.30$ to $10.72\%$ at $T=500$, although the
intermediate values fluctuate. Diffusion error is already small at the
shortest duration: its median changes from $1.11\%$ to $0.86\%$ between
these endpoints.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/results/repeated-duration.pdf}
    \caption{Exploratory duration sweep with 50 training trajectories and
    fixed observation spacing $\Delta t\approx0.03334$. Lines show median
    coefficient errors over five fits; bands show the 25th--75th percentiles.}
    \label{fig:repeated-duration}
\end{figure}

The comparison illustrates why observation spacing and total observation time
should be distinguished. Longer trajectories provide additional time over
which to observe the systematic drift, whereas denser sampling resolves
short-time fluctuations. Here, extending duration gives an overall improvement
in drift recovery that is absent when only the already fine sampling is made
denser. This is an observed tendency, not a monotonic learning curve or a
separation of the two coefficients: both depend on the data and fitting
procedure.

\paragraph{Coarse sampling and validation at the original scale.}

The coarsest configuration exposes a limitation of the fitting approach.
With 500 observations over $T=500$, training intervals are approximately
$1.00$, while validation intervals remain approximately $0.03334$.
Four of the five fits select the untrained initial checkpoint: none of their
evaluated trained checkpoints improves its fine-scale validation loss.
The selected diffusion remains at its initial value $0.9$, giving
$18.18\%$ error relative to the true amplitude $1.1$. Two extended checks
still select the untrained checkpoint after 6,000 updates. In this specific
validation sense, the untrained model performs better than the evaluated
trained models, despite having inaccurate coefficients itself.

A plausible explanation is the mismatch between a local Gaussian transition
model and increments spanning a long interval. The loss approximates the drift
and diffusion as constant within each interval. Over a coarse interval, the
state can move through regions with different drift, so the observed
displacement combines dynamics that this approximation does not resolve.
Fitting it can therefore favor coefficients that represent finite-interval
transitions rather than the original instantaneous SDE coefficients. Such a
representation need not transfer to the original fine time scale used for
validation. Coarse sampling leaves the generating SDE unchanged, but obscures
the within-interval dynamics in the observations used by this loss.

The checkpoint results demonstrate the failure to improve fine-scale
validation in these runs. They are consistent with this approximation-error
explanation, but do not isolate it from optimization effects or establish that
the trained models accurately fit the coarse transitions. The practical lesson
of these exploratory examples is that observation spacing, trajectory duration,
and the time scale assumed by the loss all matter when interpreting learned
SDE coefficients. Full experiment details and outputs remain in
\texttt{likelihood\_calc.ipynb}, \texttt{sde\_test\_data.ipynb}, and
\texttt{sandbox/experiments/experiment\_results}.

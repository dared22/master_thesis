\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

Financial time series are commonly modelled as realizations of stochastic
processes because their future evolution is uncertain \parencite{PishroNik2014}.
This section first introduces ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to describe
continuous-time dynamics and stochastic processes to describe randomness. It
then brings these ideas together in stochastic differential equations (SDEs),
which describe how a system evolves under both systematic and random
influences. For the financial data considered here, the aim is to learn these
dynamics from observed time series. SDEs can be fitted to discrete observations,
including sparsely sampled data, although sparse observations can make the
underlying dynamics difficult to identify \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.
Neural SDEs use neural networks to learn unknown dynamics from data
\parencite{Kidger2022}.
\bgroup\markoverwith{\textcolor{yellow}{\rule[-0.5ex]{2pt}{2.5ex}}}\ULon{The
section develops these concepts toward the thesis's aim of generating
equity-return paths and assessing their dependence and risk characteristics.}

\subsection{Ordinary differential equations}
\label{sec:ode-theory}

An ordinary differential equation (ODE) specifies how a state
$x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ changes over time. Together with a starting value,
it forms an initial-value problem \parencite{kohler2014elementary}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dx(t)}{dt}=f(x(t),u(t),t),\qquad x(t_0)=x_0.
    \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
The state contains the $d$ quantities describing the system, $u(t)$ is a known
input with no randomness, and $f$ gives the rate of change. We later refer to
$f$ as the \emph{vector field}: it gives the direction and rate of change at
each state for a given input and time. Integrating this rate gives the
starting state plus the accumulated change
\parencite{thomas2003thomas}:
\begin{equation}
    x(t)=x_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}f(x(s),u(s),s)\,ds.
    \label{eq:ode-integral}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Numerical approximation}

Euler's method approximates the solution by taking steps using the rate of
change at the start of each step \parencite{kohler2014elementary}:
\begin{equation}
    x_{i+1}=x_i+h_i f(x_i,u(t_i),t_i),\qquad h_i=t_{i+1}-t_i,
    \label{eq:euler-ode}
\end{equation}
where $x_i$ approximates $x(t_i)$ and $h_i$ is the step size. For example,
$dx/dt=2x$ with $x(0)=1$ has the exact solution $x(t)=e^{2t}$, while Euler
uses $x_{i+1}=(1+2h)x_i$ for a constant step size $h$.
Figure~\ref{fig:ode-euler} shows how smaller steps improve this approximation.
For sufficiently smooth solutions and small steps, halving $h$ roughly halves
the error over a fixed time interval \parencite{kohler2014elementary}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/theory/ode-euler.pdf}
    \caption{Exact solution of $dx/dt=2x$, $x(0)=1$, and Euler approximations
    with three step sizes $h$. Markers show computed values; straight lines
    connect them. The vertical line marks the error $\epsilon$ between the
    exact solution and the Euler approximation with $h=0.2$ at $t=0.8$.}
    \label{fig:ode-euler}
\end{figure}

The solver may take several steps between observations. Smaller steps can
improve numerical accuracy, but do not add data for learning the model.

\subsection{Stochastic processes}
\label{sec:stochastic-processes}

A stochastic process is a family of random variables indexed by time, written
$\{X_t:t\in\mathcal{T}\}$. At a fixed time $t$, $X_t$ is a random variable
describing the possible states at that time. One outcome of the process across
time is called a realization or sample path \parencite{PishroNik2014}.
The model therefore describes a probability distribution over possible paths,
including the distribution of values at each time and their dependence across
time \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}. An observed time series records values from
just one realization.

For a continuous-time process, the time index ranges over an interval, such as
$\mathcal{T}=[0,T]$; for a discrete-time process, it ranges over a countable set.
Continuous time alone does not require continuous sample paths
\parencite{PishroNik2014}.
If the state is observed without measurement error, the data are
\begin{equation}
    y_i=X_{t_i},\qquad i=0,\ldots,n.
    \label{eq:process-observations}
\end{equation}
The observation times may be regularly or irregularly spaced. Daily values
sample a path without revealing its within-day evolution. Generating paths
therefore requires a model for how the state evolves between observations.

\subsubsection{Brownian motion}
\label{sec:brownian-motion}

Brownian motion, also called a Wiener process, models accumulated random
changes. Standard Brownian motion $W_t$ starts at $W_0=0$ and has continuous
paths. An increment $W_{t+h}-W_t$ is the change over a time interval of length
$h$. Increments over non-overlapping intervals are independent and satisfy
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}:
\begin{equation}
    W_{t+h}-W_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,h),\qquad h>0.
    \label{eq:brownian-increments}
\end{equation}
Throughout this thesis, $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$ denotes a normal
distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Thus the increment has
mean zero, variance $h$, and standard deviation $\sqrt{h}$. Its size is
therefore scaled by $\sqrt{h}$, rather than $h$ \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.

Values of a Brownian path at chosen times can be generated by adding
independent normal increments:
\begin{equation}
    W_{t_{i+1}}=W_{t_i}+\sqrt{h_i}\,Z_i,\qquad
    h_i=t_{i+1}-t_i,\qquad Z_i\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).
    \label{eq:brownian-grid}
\end{equation}
Here the $Z_i$ are independent standard normal draws. The scaled increments
have variances $h_i$, which add to $t$ when starting at time zero. Their sum
is also normal, giving $W_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,t)$. Different draws produce
different paths of the same process \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.

Although the paths are continuous, they have no ordinary time derivative
anywhere, with probability one \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}. The SDEs below
therefore use Brownian increments to represent random changes.

\subsection{Stochastic differential equations}
\label{sec:sde-theory}

An SDE combines the rate-of-change description of an ODE with a stochastic
driving process. In the It\^o convention, a scalar diffusion driven by Brownian
motion satisfies
\begin{equation}
    dX_t=b(t,X_t)\,dt+s(t,X_t)\,dW_t,
    \label{eq:sde}
\end{equation}
where $b$ is the drift, $s$ is the diffusion amplitude, and $W_t$ is the
Brownian motion introduced in Section~\ref{sec:brownian-motion}.
The drift describes the systematic contribution to the evolution, while the
diffusion amplitude controls the strength of the random forcing
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}. The solution $X_t$ is itself a
stochastic process: each realization of the driving Brownian motion generates
a state trajectory for a given initial condition when a unique solution exists.

Since Brownian paths are not differentiable, we use the integral form
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019},
\begin{equation}
    X_t=X_0+\int_0^t b(u,X_u)\,du
        +\int_0^t s(u,X_u)\,dW_u.
    \label{eq:sde-integral}
\end{equation}

Measurements may also contain noise. A simple observation model is
\begin{equation}
    x_{\mathrm{obs}}(t_i)=X_{t_i}+\epsilon_i,
    \label{eq:noisy-observations}
\end{equation}
where $X_{t_i}$ is the underlying state and $\epsilon_i$ is measurement noise.
This noise affects the recorded value, while the Brownian term in an SDE
affects how the state evolves. An ODE can therefore have noisy observations
without its dynamics being stochastic. A model can also include both
stochastic dynamics and measurement noise \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.

\subsubsection{Euler--Maruyama and local increments}

Euler--Maruyama adds a random change to each Euler step
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}:
\begin{equation}
    X_{i+1}=X_i+b(t_i,X_i)h_i
       +s(t_i,X_i)\sqrt{h_i}\,Z_i,
    \qquad Z_i\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).
    \label{eq:euler-maruyama}
\end{equation}
Here $X_i$ approximates $X_{t_i}$, $h_i=t_{i+1}-t_i$ is the step size,
and the $Z_i$ are independent standard normal draws.

Let $\Delta X_i=X_{t_{i+1}}-X_{t_i}$ denote the state change over an interval.
The notation $\Delta X_i\mid X_{t_i}=x$ means this change \emph{given that}
the state at the start is $x$. Treating $b$ and $s$ as constant over a short
interval gives \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}
\begin{equation}
    \Delta X_i\mid X_{t_i}=x
       \approx\mathcal{N}\!\left(b(t_i,x)h_i,s(t_i,x)^2h_i\right).
    \label{eq:local-gaussian}
\end{equation}
The mean change is $b(t_i,x)h_i$ and its variance is $s(t_i,x)^2h_i$.
This distribution is exact for one Euler--Maruyama step, but generally
approximate for the SDE itself. Over a long observation interval, $b$ and $s$
may change substantially, making this approximation less reliable.

\subsubsection{Example: a randomly forced oscillator}
\label{sec:oscillator-theory}

A simple SDE example is an oscillator whose velocity receives random changes
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}:
\begin{equation}
    dX_t=V_t\,dt,\qquad
    dV_t=-\omega_0^2X_t\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t.
    \label{eq:stochastic-oscillator}
\end{equation}
Here $X_t$ is position and $V_t$ is velocity. The term $-\omega_0^2X_t$
pulls the system back towards zero, producing oscillations. The parameter
$\omega_0$ controls their speed, while $\sigma$ controls the strength of the
random changes in velocity.

Figure~\ref{fig:oscillator-paths} shows ten paths simulated with
Euler--Maruyama from the same starting state. Their differences arise from
the random forcing of velocity; no measurement noise is added.

\subsection{Neural stochastic differential equations}
\label{sec:neural-sde-theory}

Neural differential equations use neural networks to represent unknown
coefficient functions. The deterministic neural ODE provides a starting point
for introducing neural drift and diffusion functions in an SDE
\parencite{Kidger2022}.

\subsubsection{Neural networks}
\label{sec:neural-network-background}

A neural network is a parameterized function, written $f_\theta$, where
$\theta$ collects its trainable weights and biases. Training learns these
parameters from data by minimizing a loss, which measures how well the model
fits the data \parencite{Goodfellow2016}. In a neural SDE, networks can
represent the unknown drift
$b_\theta$ and, if needed, the diffusion $s_\theta$, allowing these functions
to be learned from observations \parencite{Kidger2022}.

This thesis assumes some familiarity with neural networks and deep learning,
including layers, activation functions, and gradient-based training. The focus
here is on using networks within differential equations.

\subsubsection{Neural ordinary differential equations}
\label{sec:neural-ode-theory}

A neural ODE replaces a prescribed vector field by a parameterized neural
network \parencite{Chen2018}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dz(t)}{dt}=f_\theta(t,z(t)),\qquad z(t_0)=z_0.
    \label{eq:neural-ode}
\end{equation}
In the earlier example $dx/dt=2x$, the rate $2x$ was specified in advance.
Replacing it with $f_\theta(t,x)$ makes the rate a function learned from data.
A numerical solver produces a trajectory for each choice of $\theta$;
training adjusts these parameters to fit observed trajectories, for example
by minimizing squared differences between predicted and observed states
\parencite{Chen2018}. With fixed parameters and initial state, the resulting
neural ODE remains deterministic.

\subsubsection{From neural ODEs to neural SDEs}

A neural SDE parameterizes the drift and, where required, the diffusion:
\begin{equation}
    dX_t=b_\theta(t,X_t)\,dt+s_\theta(t,X_t)\,dW_t.
    \label{eq:neural-sde}
\end{equation}
Each independent realization has its own Brownian forcing. Neural networks
provide flexible coefficient functions, but their parameterization must still
respect the conditions needed for a well-defined SDE
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}. Neural differential
equations and their stochastic extensions are developed in
\textcite{Kidger2022}.

\subsubsection{Likelihood training from observed increments}

One way to train a neural SDE is to make the observed changes likely under
the normal approximation in Equation~\eqref{eq:local-gaussian}. Here we
assume that the state is observed without measurement noise and that the
diffusion $s_\theta$ is positive \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.

For an observed change $\Delta X_i$, the predicted mean change is
$b_\theta(X_i)h_i$. Their difference,
$r_i=\Delta X_i-b_\theta(X_i)h_i$, is the residual. Taking the negative
logarithm of the normal density gives the training loss
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019,NISTHandbook}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_i(\theta)
       =\frac{r_i^2}{2s_\theta(X_i)^2h_i}
        +\log s_\theta(X_i)
        +\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi h_i).
    \label{eq:neural-sde-nll}
\end{equation}
The first term penalizes prediction errors relative to the model's noise
scale. The second discourages making the diffusion large simply to hide
those errors. The final term does not depend on the parameters, so it can be
omitted during training. We adjust the parameters to reduce the average loss
over observed changes. Each change is assessed given its starting state;
consecutive states need not be independent \parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.

The example below uses this training rule; the financial model's training
method remains a separate choice. Noisy or incomplete observations require
a suitable observation model or another inference method
\parencite{SarkkaSolin2019}.

\subsubsection{Illustration: observation spacing}
\label{sec:neural-example}
\label{sec:observation-spacing-example}

Consider the periodic toy SDE
\begin{equation}
    dX_t=-\sin(X_t)\,dt+1.1\,dW_t,\qquad X_0=0.
    \label{eq:periodic-toy-sde}
\end{equation}
A neural drift and positive constant diffusion are fitted using
Equation~\eqref{eq:neural-sde-nll}. Figure~\ref{fig:neural-sde-paths} shows
50 new paths from one fitted model. They share the same starting state and
learned parameters, but use different Brownian increments.

The plot shows sensitivity to observation spacing in this example: diffusion
recovery improves overall with finer sampling, while drift error does not
improve monotonically. A plausible contributor at coarse spacing is approximation
error: the local Gaussian transition treats coefficients as constant over
an interval in which the state and drift may change substantially. The
experiment does not establish this as the cause or isolate it from
optimization and checkpoint-selection effects. The network structure, training
settings, error definitions, duration comparison, and coarse-sampling checkpoint
results are given in Appendix~\ref{app:periodic-toy-study}. This preliminary
illustration does not answer the equity-return research question.

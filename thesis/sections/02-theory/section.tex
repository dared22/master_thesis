\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

Differential equations describe how a system changes through time. Ordinary
differential equations specify a deterministic rate of change, while stochastic
differential equations add random forcing. Neural differential equations retain
this continuous-time structure and use neural networks to represent unknown
coefficients. This section develops these models in that order and derives the
likelihood expressions used in the experiments in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Ordinary differential equations}
\label{sec:ode-theory}

An ordinary differential equation (ODE) relates a state to its time derivative.
For a state $x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, an initial-value problem takes the form
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dx(t)}{dt}=f(t,x(t)),\qquad x(t_0)=x_0.
    \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
The vector field $f$ specifies the instantaneous direction and speed of motion.
The equation is autonomous when $f$ has no explicit time dependence. Its
integral form is
\begin{equation}
    x(t)=x_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds.
    \label{eq:ode-integral}
\end{equation}
If $f$ is continuous in time and locally Lipschitz in the state, the
initial-value problem has a unique local solution. Additional conditions,
such as a suitable linear-growth bound, prevent finite-time blow-up and permit
extension to a finite observation horizon. Under uniqueness, the initial state
and vector field determine the trajectory. A general treatment of ODEs and
their neural extensions is given by \textcite{Kidger2022}.

\subsubsection{Numerical approximation}

A numerical solver approximates the solution at discrete times. Explicit Euler
uses
\begin{equation}
    x_{i+1}=x_i+h_i f(t_i,x_i),\qquad h_i=t_{i+1}-t_i.
    \label{eq:euler-ode}
\end{equation}
For sufficiently smooth solutions on a fixed finite interval, its global error
is of order $h$ for a uniform step size $h$. Accuracy also depends on numerical
stability.

The integration step and the observation interval serve different purposes.
The integration step controls numerical approximation, while the observation
interval determines which state values enter statistical estimation. Several
integration steps can occur between observations. This distinction matters
when assessing the effect of observation density.

\subsection{Stochastic differential equations}
\label{sec:sde-theory}

Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) represent dynamics driven by random
forcing. In the It\^o convention, a scalar diffusion satisfies
\begin{equation}
    dX_t=b(t,X_t)\,dt+s(t,X_t)\,dW_t,
    \label{eq:sde}
\end{equation}
where $b$ is the drift, $s$ is the diffusion amplitude, and $W_t$ is standard
Brownian motion.
The stochastic integral and numerical approximation follow the framework
described by \textcite{Higham2001} and \textcite{Kidger2022}.

\subsubsection{Brownian motion}

Standard Brownian motion starts at zero, has continuous paths, and has
independent Gaussian increments:
\begin{equation}
    W_{t+h}-W_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,h),\qquad h>0.
    \label{eq:brownian-increments}
\end{equation}
Its paths are almost surely nowhere differentiable. Consequently,
Equation~\eqref{eq:sde} is interpreted through an integral equation,
\begin{equation}
    X_t=X_0+\int_0^t b(u,X_u)\,du
        +\int_0^t s(u,X_u)\,dW_u.
    \label{eq:sde-integral}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Euler--Maruyama and local increments}

Euler--Maruyama extends explicit Euler by adding a Gaussian noise increment:
\begin{equation}
    X_{i+1}=X_i+b(t_i,X_i)h_i
       +s(t_i,X_i)\sqrt{h_i}\,Z_i,
    \qquad Z_i\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).
    \label{eq:euler-maruyama}
\end{equation}
Under suitable assumptions, Euler--Maruyama has strong convergence order $1/2$
and weak convergence order one \parencite{Higham2001}. Strong convergence
measures path accuracy under the same Brownian forcing, while weak convergence
measures accuracy in averages of functions of the state.

Freezing the coefficients over a short observation interval gives
\begin{equation}
    \Delta X_i\mid X_i=x
       \approx\mathcal{N}\!\left(b(t_i,x)h_i,s(t_i,x)^2h_i\right).
    \label{eq:local-gaussian}
\end{equation}
The Gaussian transition approximation treats the coefficients as constant over
each observation interval. It is exact for a single Euler--Maruyama update,
but generally approximate for the underlying SDE. Over shorter intervals, the
drift contribution becomes smaller relative to the noise, so denser sampling
does not necessarily improve drift recovery.

\subsubsection{Probability densities}

\paragraph{Brownian motion.}
For Brownian motion with constant diffusion amplitude $\sigma>0$, no drift,
and a fixed initial state $X_0=x_0$, the state at time $T>0$ is
\begin{equation}
    X_T=x_0+\sigma W_T,\qquad
    X_T\sim\mathcal{N}(x_0,\sigma^2T).
\end{equation}
The distribution is normal, with mean $x_0$ and variance $\sigma^2T$.
Thus, endpoints of many independent trajectories form an approximately
bell-shaped histogram centered around the initial state. Its spread increases
with time, with standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{T}$.

\subsubsection{Brownian diffusion and maximum likelihood}
\label{sec:brownian-mle-theory}

For $dX_t=\sigma\,dW_t$ with $X_0=0$, $X_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2T)$.
For $N$ independent paths observed at the same final time, maximum likelihood
gives
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{end}}^2
      =\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(X_T^{(j)}\right)^2.
    \label{eq:brownian-endpoint-mle}
\end{equation}
The zero mean is known, so the expression uses the second moment about zero.
For one path with $m$ equally spaced increments of length $h$, the
log-likelihood is
\begin{equation}
    \ell(\sigma)
      =-\frac{m}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2h)
       -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2h}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(\Delta X_i)^2.
    \label{eq:brownian-loglikelihood}
\end{equation}
Differentiating with respect to $\sigma^2$ yields
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{inc}}^2
       =\frac{1}{mh}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(\Delta X_i)^2.
    \label{eq:brownian-increment-mle}
\end{equation}
Both variance estimators are unbiased in this model; taking a square root does
not preserve unbiasedness. Their observed accuracy depends on the number of
independent Gaussian quantities entering the estimate.

\paragraph{Alternative derivation using variance.}
The endpoint estimator can also be obtained from the identity
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Var}(X_T)
      =\mathbb{E}[X_T^2]-\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X_T]\bigr)^2.
\end{equation}
For the same Brownian model with $X_0=0$, the mean is zero and the variance
is $\sigma^2T$. Substituting these values gives
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^2T=\mathbb{E}[X_T^2]-0^2,
    \qquad
    \sigma^2=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_T^2]}{T}.
\end{equation}
Replacing the expected squared endpoint by its average over $N$ independent
trajectories gives
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\sigma}^2
      =\frac{1}{T}\left(\frac{1}{N}
         \sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(X_T^{(j)}\right)^2\right).
\end{equation}
This moment-based derivation gives the same estimator as maximum likelihood
in Equation~\eqref{eq:brownian-endpoint-mle}. The mean is known to be zero,
so it is not replaced by the sample mean, which can differ from zero in a
finite dataset.

\subsubsection{The randomly forced harmonic oscillator}
\label{sec:oscillator-theory}

A harmonic oscillator with random forcing of its velocity satisfies
\begin{equation}
    dX_t=V_t\,dt,\qquad
    dV_t=-\omega_0^2X_t\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t.
    \label{eq:stochastic-oscillator}
\end{equation}
Here $X_t$ is position, $V_t$ is velocity, $\omega_0>0$ is the natural angular
frequency, and $\sigma>0$ is the diffusion amplitude.
Integrating the velocity equation and removing the restoring-force
contribution gives
\begin{equation}
    R_T=V_T-V_0+\omega_0^2\int_0^T X_u\,du
       =\sigma W_T.
    \label{eq:oscillator-corrected-total}
\end{equation}
Thus $R_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2T)$, and $N$ independent corrected totals
give
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\sigma}_{R}^{\,2}
      =\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(R_T^{(j)}\right)^2.
    \label{eq:oscillator-total-mle}
\end{equation}
This is the likelihood estimate based on corrected totals. For Euler data,
the left-endpoint sum $h\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}X_i$ makes the correction exact for the
discrete updates, up to floating-point error. Alternatively, retaining every
corrected velocity increment
$\Delta V_i+\omega_0^2X_i h$ gives an increment-based likelihood with more
observations than one total per path.
For $N$ Euler paths with $m$ equally spaced steps, this estimator is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{kick}}^2
      =\frac{1}{Nmh}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}
       \left(\Delta V_i^{(j)}+\omega_0^2X_i^{(j)}h\right)^2.
    \label{eq:oscillator-increment-mle}
\end{equation}
It uses individual corrected increments before squaring, whereas
Equation~\eqref{eq:oscillator-total-mle} first sums the increments within
each path. These retain different amounts of information about diffusion.

For zero initial conditions, linearity gives
\begin{align}
    X_T&=\frac{\sigma}{\omega_0}
       \int_0^T\sin(\omega_0(T-u))\,dW_u,\\
    V_T&=\sigma\int_0^T\cos(\omega_0(T-u))\,dW_u.
\end{align}
These variables are jointly Gaussian with zero means. Their variances and
covariance are
\begin{align}
    \operatorname{Var}(X_T)
       &=\frac{\sigma^2}{\omega_0^2}
         \left(\frac{T}{2}-\frac{\sin(2\omega_0T)}{4\omega_0}\right),
         \label{eq:oscillator-position-variance}\\
    \operatorname{Var}(V_T)
       &=\sigma^2
         \left(\frac{T}{2}+\frac{\sin(2\omega_0T)}{4\omega_0}\right),\\
    \operatorname{Cov}(X_T,V_T)
       &=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\omega_0^2}\sin^2(\omega_0T).
\end{align}
Position and velocity are therefore generally dependent. These expressions
describe the continuous model.
The oscillator is undamped. It\^o's formula gives
$d\mathbb{E}[E_t]/dt=\sigma^2/2$, so its expected energy increases even in the
continuous model. This physical growth is distinct from Euler's additional
numerical energy growth.

\subsection{Neural ordinary differential equations}
\label{sec:neural-ode-theory}

A neural ODE replaces a prescribed vector field by a parameterized neural
network \parencite{Chen2018}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dz(t)}{dt}=f_\theta(t,z(t)),\qquad z(t_0)=z_0.
    \label{eq:neural-ode}
\end{equation}
The parameters $\theta$ determine the dynamics, while a numerical solver
evaluates the trajectory. Euler discretization gives
$z_{i+1}=z_i+h_i f_\theta(t_i,z_i)$, connecting the model to residual updates.
The continuous model separates the vector field from the solver's time grid.

For fully observed deterministic trajectories $y_j$ at times $t_j$, one
possible objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{J}(\theta)
       =\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
          \|z_\theta(t_j)-y_j\|^2.
    \label{eq:neural-ode-objective}
\end{equation}
Observation likelihoods or regularization terms can replace or augment this
objective. Training differentiates a loss through the dynamics. This can be
done through numerical solver operations or through sensitivity equations.
For a terminal loss $\Phi(z(T))$, the continuous adjoint satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \frac{da}{dt}
       =-\left(\frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}\right)^{\mathsf T}a,
    \qquad a(T)=\nabla_z\Phi(z(T)).
    \label{eq:neural-ode-adjoint}
\end{equation}
Numerical choices affect computed trajectories and gradients; continuous and
discretized sensitivity calculations need not coincide exactly
\parencite{Chen2018,Kidger2022}.

With fixed parameters and initial state, a neural ODE remains deterministic.
Random initial conditions or a separate observation model can introduce
uncertainty, but the vector field alone does not add random forcing during
evolution. The present experiments do not train a neural ODE baseline; this
model supplies the conceptual bridge to neural SDEs.

\subsection{Neural stochastic differential equations}
\label{sec:neural-sde-theory}

A neural SDE parameterizes the drift and, where required, the diffusion:
\begin{equation}
    dX_t=b_\theta(t,X_t)\,dt+s_\theta(t,X_t)\,dW_t.
    \label{eq:neural-sde}
\end{equation}
Each independent realization has its own Brownian forcing. Neural networks
provide flexible coefficient functions, but their parameterization must still
respect the conditions needed for a well-defined SDE. Neural differential
equations and their stochastic extensions are developed in
\textcite{Kidger2022}.

\subsubsection{Structure used in the experiments}

For the periodic-potential experiment, the drift network receives
$(\sin x,\cos x)$ as inputs. This enforces the known period $2\pi$, appropriate
to the generating drift $b(x)=-\sin x$. Its four hidden layers each contain 64 units
with hyperbolic-tangent activation. The architecture learns the shape of the
periodic drift while incorporating prior knowledge of its period.

The active diffusion model is a single learned positive scalar,
\begin{equation}
    s_\theta(x)=\operatorname{softplus}(\rho)+\varepsilon,
    \qquad \operatorname{softplus}(\rho)=\log(1+e^\rho),
    \qquad \varepsilon=10^{-3}.
    \label{eq:positive-diffusion}
\end{equation}
Thus the experiments learn a neural drift and constant diffusion. Positivity
avoids the sign ambiguity of a scalar diffusion amplitude when identifying its
law through $s_\theta^2$.

\subsubsection{Likelihood training from observed increments}

For each observed increment $\Delta X_i$, the model predicts a normal
distribution with mean $b_\theta(X_i)h_i$ and variance $s_\theta(X_i)^2h_i$,
as in Equation~\eqref{eq:local-gaussian}. Evaluating its probability density
at the observed increment gives the likelihood of that increment given its
starting state. Training adjusts the parameters to increase this likelihood.
Equivalently, we minimize its negative logarithm. Writing the difference
between the observed and predicted increment as
$r_i=\Delta X_i-b_\theta(X_i)h_i$, this gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_i(\theta)
       =\frac{r_i^2}{2s_\theta(X_i)^2h_i}
        +\log s_\theta(X_i)
        +\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi h_i).
    \label{eq:neural-sde-nll}
\end{equation}
Taking the negative logarithm of the normal density turns its exponential
into the squared-residual term. Its normalizing factor gives
$\log s_\theta(X_i)+\tfrac12\log(2\pi h_i)$. The implementation averages
the first two terms in Equation~\eqref{eq:neural-sde-nll}, omitting the final
term because it does not depend on the learned parameters and therefore
does not affect training. Actual elapsed times $h_i$ enter the loss,
including when selecting a subset of saved observations. Consecutive
increments form conditional transition factors through the Markov property;
their starting states are not assumed to be independent.

The residual term measures agreement with the local drift and noise scale.
The $\log s_\theta(X_i)$ term prevents arbitrarily increasing diffusion to reduce the
standardized residual. Drift and diffusion are therefore coupled in training.
Adam performs parameter updates using randomly sampled minibatches.
The data-size experiments select a checkpoint using a fixed sample of
held-out increments. This fitting strategy assumes direct state observations
without measurement noise; noisy or partially observed states require a
different observation model or inference procedure.

\subsubsection{Checking the learned model}

Because the data are simulated, we know the true drift and diffusion. We can
therefore compare them directly with the learned values. To check the drift,
we evaluate both functions at the same $Q$ evenly spaced positions $x_q$
covering one period. The percentage errors are
\begin{equation}
    E_b=100\sqrt{
       \frac{\sum_{q=1}^{Q}[b_\theta(x_q)-b(x_q)]^2}
            {\sum_{q=1}^{Q}b(x_q)^2}},\qquad
    E_\sigma=100\frac{|\widehat{\sigma}-\sigma|}{\sigma}.
    \label{eq:coefficient-errors}
\end{equation}
Here $b$ is the true drift, $b_\theta$ is the learned drift, $\sigma$ is the
true diffusion amplitude, and $\widehat{\sigma}$ is its learned value.
The drift error $E_b$ combines the differences across the sampled positions
and expresses their size relative to the true drift. The diffusion error
$E_\sigma$ is the difference between the learned and true amplitudes,
relative to the true amplitude. Zero means an exact match at the evaluated
positions for drift, or an exact match of the amplitude for diffusion.
These relative errors require at least one nonzero true drift value on the
evaluation grid and $\sigma>0$.

We also check the loss on observations not used for training and compare
the final positions of simulated trajectories. These checks answer different
questions: the loss measures how well the model explains observed increments,
while the final positions show its behavior over a longer time. A low
validation loss or similar final-position distributions do not by themselves
guarantee that the learned drift and diffusion are correct. In particular,
at short observation intervals, drift errors can have little effect on the
loss because their contribution is small relative to the noise.

The amount of data also needs to be described carefully. With $M$ trajectories
and $K$ observations per trajectory, there are $M(K-1)$ observed increments.
However, the same increment count can come from densely sampled short paths
or sparsely sampled long paths. These need not be equally useful for learning
the coefficients, so the experiments distinguish observation spacing,
trajectory duration, and the number of independent trajectories.
